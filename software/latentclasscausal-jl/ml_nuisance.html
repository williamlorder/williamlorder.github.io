Machine-learning nuisance models

Both estimators need two nuisance functions: a propensity model and an outcome model. For intervene_membership these are P(A = 1 | X) and the latent class regression P(C = k | A, X). For intervene_effect they are the propensity chain π(s | H) and the outcome regression μ(s, H). The propensity and outcome keywords take :linear or :quadratic, which fit the parametric models of the theory pages, or a learner. The learner can be given by name or as a NuisanceLearner object:

using LatentClassCausal
intervene_membership(m, A, X; propensity = :superlearner, outcome = :superlearner)
intervene_effect(Y, A, X, [1, 2], setto([0, 0]);
                    outcome = :forest, propensity = :lasso_quadratic)
# a custom super learner library
intervene_effect(Y, A, X, [1, 2], setto([0, 0]); outcome = :superlearner,
                    propensity = :superlearner,
                    learners = [:glm, :lasso_quadratic, ForestLearner(ntrees = 300)])

AIPW and TMLE stay doubly robust with learners. Their influence-function standard errors remain valid when both nuisances converge fast enough that the product of their L² errors is o(n^{-1/2}) (Chernozhukov et al. 2018). Cross-fitting is what allows flexible learners such as forests without Donsker conditions. Every learner is fitted only within the training folds of the estimator's folds-fold split and predicts the held-out fold. Learners never see the held-out rows: internal cross-validation (λ of the lasso, the super learner's meta-learning) is nested inside the training folds.

:superlearnerSuperLearner(library; nfolds = 5, meta = :nnls)stacked ensemble of library

The super learner (van der Laan, Polley & Hubbard 2007; Polley & van der Laan 2010) fits each library member on V − 1 folds of the training data and predicts the remaining fold. The meta-learner then combines these cross-validated predictions. meta = :nnls uses non-negative least squares rescaled to a convex combination, the default of the R package SuperLearner. meta = :nnloglik (binary responses) uses non-negative logistic stacking of the logits. The default library (default_library) is the mean, the main-effects GLM, the lasso, the lasso on squares and pairwise products, and a random forest. It uses only built-in learners, so results do not depend on which packages happen to be loaded. Add BoostLearner() explicitly after using EvoTrees. A fitted super learner shows its weights and cross-validated risks:

f = fit_learner(SuperLearner(), X, y; family = :binomial)
f            # table of learners, weights, CV risks
predict_learner(f, Xnew)

How each estimator uses learners.

  • intervene_membership, propensity: one probability fit of A on X.
  • intervene_membership, outcome: the latent class C is not observed. A learner therefore estimates P(ĉ = j | A = a, X), the distribution of the modal assignment, separately in each arm with K one-vs-rest probability fits that are then normalised. Measurement invariance gives P(ĉ = j | a, X) = Σₖ P(C = k | a, X) D[k, j], so the class regression is P(C | a, X) = P(ĉ | a, X) D⁻¹. Negative entries are set to zero and the rows renormalised. This plays the role of the misclassification-corrected three-step regression lcreg_fit used by :linear/:quadratic.
  • intervene_effect, propensity: each link P(Aj | A{S,<j}, H) of the chain is a probability fit, so a chain of binary learners gives the joint (multinomial) distribution of the intervened exposures.
  • intervene_effect, outcome: when every exposure pattern of AS has at least 20 rows in the training fold, the learner is fitted separately within each pattern (a "T-learner", as in the interactive regression model of double machine learning) and μ(s, H) is that pattern's prediction. Otherwise, for example with many intervened exposures, one pooled fit of Y on [AS, H] is evaluated at every pattern, with AS unpenalised in penalised learners. The pooled fit is unsafe for trees: when H dominates the outcome, splits on AS are rare. On the Kang–Schafer outcome below, a pooled forest's plug-in recovered 1.6 of a true effect of 10, and the per-arm forest recovered 6.1.
  • bound (default 0.01): probabilities predicted by learners are bounded to [bound, 1 − bound] for the propensity score of intervene_membership and for every conditional probability of the chain, so the joint probabilities still sum to one. Forests and boosting can otherwise return exactly 0 or 1. The parametric paths are never bounded (trim of intervene_membership applies to both).

Reproducibility. Learners draw random numbers (CV folds, bootstrap samples) only from generators derived from seed, the fold and the nuisance model. They never use the generator that assigns the cross-fitting folds, so results are reproducible. Forests are deterministic whatever the number of threads. The :linear/:quadratic code paths are unchanged except for one thing: logistic regressions now use analytic Newton steps (gradient D'(y − p), Hessian −D'WD) instead of automatic differentiation. The iteration is the same, and on a snapshot of 62 results of intervene_membership, intervene_effect and the latent-class extensions the estimates agree with the earlier version to a relative 5 × 10⁻¹³. A binomial TMLE on 121,000 rows with 35 adjustment columns now takes 29 s instead of 940 s, and a g-formula with B = 200 on 3,000 rows 9.7 s instead of 120 s (both measured when the bootstrap did not yet cross-fit its replicates). With the current bootstrap, which refits every replicate over the same 5 folds as the estimate, a g-formula with B = 200 on 3,000 rows takes 14.8 s on one thread of a Nibi node, against 14.3 s with folds = 1 (validation/timing_gformula.jl, job 28125685). Only under complete separation, where no MLE exists, do the two diverge (|β| about 10⁴ against about 200 before). Both give fitted probabilities of 0 or 1 there.

Elastic net details. The coordinate descent works on standardised columns with covariance updates. The relative convergence threshold is 10⁻⁷ times the null deviance, and IRLS steps for the binomial family stop when the penalised deviance changes by less than 10⁻⁸ relatively. The path stops early once the residual deviance changes by less than a fraction 10⁻⁵ between successive λ. glmnet's absolute rules (deviance ratio

0.999, or a change in the ratio < 10⁻⁵) were not used: they stop too early when R² is

close to one, and in the Kang–Schafer outcome (R² ≈ 0.999) that biased AIPW by −0.025.

Without cross-fitting (folds ≤ 1) a warning is issued: the influence-function standard errors assume cross-fitted (or Donsker-class) nuisances. The g-formula bootstrap with a learner outcome model also has no general validity guarantee. It warns, and AIPW or TMLE should be preferred.

Simulation: Kang–Schafer misspecification

A Kang & Schafer (2007)-style simulation is provided to evaluate the learners (validation/dgp_ml.jl, validation/sim_ml.jl, validation/aggregate_ml.jl, with Slurm scripts in jobs/): the analyst observes either the correct covariates Z or the transformed covariates X, and AIPW/TMLE (intervene_effect) and AIPW (intervene_membership) are run with linear, quadratic, lasso, random-forest, boosting and super-learner nuisance models. Results are not reported here. In pilot runs, all learners reduced but did not remove the bias under the transformed covariates, so flexible nuisance models are not a substitute for measuring the right confounders.
